Charts need more colours than an interface does, and they use them for a different job. The
`data-visualization.*` tokens are a separate group for that job: they help a reader tell series apart
and read an amount from a colour. No EDS component binds them, and they are not meant for buttons,
banners or other interface elements. Token names below use Figma's dotted form;
[Getting Started](./getting_started.mdx#one-name-in-figma-css-and-typescript) shows the CSS and
TypeScript forms.

Place charts on `background.surface`. Every contrast figure on this page is measured against it, in
both colour schemes.

## Choose a group

The kind of data decides the group:

| Data | Group | Tokens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Categories with no order between them | Categorical | `data-visualization.cat.1` to `cat.10`, each with steps `1` to `5` | Production per field, incidents per region |
| Ordered values that run from low to high | Sequential | `data-visualization.seq.1` to `seq.7` | Utilisation in a heatmap, depth on a map |
| Values that run in two directions from a midpoint | Diverging | `data-visualization.div.1` to `div.9` | Above and below plan, change since last year |

Use one group per chart. When a chart mixes groups, the reader has to guess whether a colour stands
for a category or for an amount.

## Categorical colours

There are ten categorical hues, `cat.1` to `cat.10`, with five steps each. Use the hues to tell series
apart. Use the steps within one hue when a single series needs more than one shade, for example a
forecast next to the measured values, or a selected bar among the others.

### What the step numbers mean

Compare the light and dark columns in the table below. In light, step 1 is a pale tint and step 5 is
almost black. In dark it is the other way round: step 1 is a deep shade and step 5 is pale. In both
schemes, step 1 is the quietest step, sitting closest to the colour of the page, and step 5 is the one
that stands out most.

A step number therefore describes how strongly a colour stands out from the background. When this
page says "use step 1 for a tint behind the plot area", you get a tint that stays in the background in
both schemes, and "use step 5 for the one mark that must stand out" gives a mark that stands out in
both.

<DataVizStepSwap />

Step 3 is the base colour of each hue and looks the same in both schemes. The other steps swap places
around it: the pale tint that is step 1 in light is step 5 in dark. The interface colours work the
same way, as [the palette](./palette.mdx#what-the-step-numbers-mean) explains.

Because step 3 looks the same in both schemes, it does not stand out equally in both. In row 3 of the
table, the dark teal of `cat.1` (the first swatch) is strong on the light surface and almost
disappears on the dark one, and the yellow of `cat.2` (the second swatch) does the opposite.
[Contrast](#contrast) lists the hues this affects.

### Which step to use

| Step | Use it for | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Decorative areas that carry no meaning, such as a hover background or a tint behind the plot area | Closest to the background, and below 3:1 for every hue in both schemes |
| 2 | Bands that carry meaning, such as a target range or an uncertainty band, always with a label; filled marks that should step back, such as the other bars when one is selected. Not for lines or points | Reaches 3:1 for only 2 of 10 hues in light and 4 of 10 in dark, so it needs a label |
| 3 | The default colour for bars, areas and other filled marks | The anchor of the hue, the same in both schemes |
| 4 | Lines, points and a series that should stand out | Reaches 3:1 for every hue in both schemes |
| 5 | The one line or mark that has to stand out most, such as a target series | Furthest from the background in both schemes. Not for several series at once: at step 5 most of the first five hues look alike |

Where a hue is too weak in a scheme at the step it is used for, make its values available as text as
well: direct labels, a tooltip or a data table. [Contrast](#contrast) lists those hues.

### Series order and how many series

Assign hues in token order, starting at `cat.1`, and keep each series on the same hue when a filter
changes what the chart shows.

Colour alone tells up to five series apart. `cat.1` to `cat.5` at step 3 stay distinguishable with
normal colour vision and with the three common forms of colour blindness (protanopia, deuteranopia
and tritanopia). Step 3 has the same values in both schemes, so this is true in light and dark.

From six series, add a second cue. Pairs start to look alike from the sixth hue: pink `cat.4` and
purple `cat.6` are close even with full colour vision, and at seven and eight hues, `cat.1` and
`cat.7` (dark teal and red) and `cat.4` and `cat.8` (pink and green-teal) merge for readers with red
or green colour blindness. No set of eight hues can be told apart by colour alone. A chart with six or
more series can still use all ten hues if each series also has one of these:

- A direct label at the end of the line or on the bar, instead of only a legend
- A marker shape or a line dash
- A place in an "Other" group, for the smallest series

These results come from simulating colour blindness with the model by Machado, Oliveira and
Fernandes (2009) and with the check in the EDS palette generator, and counting a pair as distinct only
when it passes both.

## Sequential and diverging colours

Sequential and diverging ramps follow the same rule as the categorical steps: the distance from the
background carries the meaning. In both schemes `seq.1` sits closest to the background and stands for
the lowest values, and `seq.7` sits furthest from it and stands for the highest. In the diverging ramp
the midpoint `div.5` sits closest to the background and stands for no difference, and the two ends,
the blue `div.1` and the orange `div.9`, sit furthest from it.

These two ramps are generated separately for each scheme, so the dark values are not the light values
in reverse order.

The low end of the sequential ramp and the middle of the diverging ramp are close to invisible on the
surface, especially in dark, where `seq.1` and `div.4` to `div.6` score APCA Lc 0. That is intended,
because low values and values near the midpoint should recede in a heatmap or a map. The contrast goal
for chart marks therefore does not apply to cells in a sequential or diverging fill. A reader still has
to tell "low", "zero" and "no data" apart:

- Give cells at the low end or the midpoint a thin border, so that their shape stays visible.
- Show missing data with its own pattern or label, never with the low-end colour.
- Make exact values available another way: labels, a tooltip or a table.

## Light and dark

Most data-visualisation values change between schemes: 56 of the 66 values are different, and only
the categorical step 3 values stay the same. A chart keeps its meaning when the scheme changes,
because each step keeps its position relative to the background, but it does not keep its exact
colours. Check every chart in both schemes, and use the tokens rather than the hex values of one
scheme.

## Contrast

Chart marks are graphical objects, so WCAG 2.1 asks for a contrast of at least 3:1 against the
colours next to them when a reader needs the mark to understand the chart (success criterion 1.4.11).
EDS designs for APCA and treats WCAG as the minimum, as it does for text (see the
[colour introduction](./intro.mdx#contrast)). For chart marks:

| Mark | Minimum | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bars, areas and other filled marks | WCAG 3:1 | APCA Lc 30 |
| Lines and small markers | WCAG 3:1 | APCA Lc 45 |

Measured against `background.surface`:

| Categorical step | Light: reaches 3:1 | Light: APCA Lc | Dark: reaches 3:1 | Dark: APCA Lc |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0 of 10 hues | 10 to 35 | 0 of 10 hues | 0 to 10 |
| 2 | 2 of 10 hues | 18 to 69 | 4 of 10 hues | 0 to 38 |
| 3 | 6 of 10 hues | 26 to 94 | 9 of 10 hues | 9 to 77 |
| 4 | 10 of 10 hues | 64 to 99 | 10 of 10 hues | 33 to 86 |
| 5 | 10 of 10 hues | 93 to 102 | 10 of 10 hues | 68 to 94 |

These hues miss the minimum or the goal at the step they are used for. Make their values available as
text as well, with direct labels, a tooltip or a data table:

| Use | Light | Dark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step 3 fills, below 3:1 | `cat.2`, `cat.3`, `cat.8`, `cat.10` | `cat.1` |
| Step 3 fills, above 3:1 but below Lc 30 | none | `cat.6` (Lc 22), `cat.7` (Lc 20) |
| Step 4 lines, below Lc 45 | none | `cat.1` (Lc 33), `cat.7` (Lc 43) |

In dark the two measures disagree more than in light. `cat.7.3` reaches 3.13:1 but only Lc 20, so it
passes the minimum while still looking faint on the dark surface.

### Text in charts

Use the `text.*` tokens for axis labels, data labels and legends. The `data-visualization.*` values
are tuned for marks and do not reach the contrast text needs on every step. When a data label sits on
a coloured bar, check its contrast against the bar rather than against the surface.

### Colour is not the only cue

Some readers cannot tell certain hues apart, and screenshots and printouts can lose colour. Pair
colour with a second cue: direct labels, marker shapes, line dashes, or a table with the same data.
The [accessibility foundation](../accessibility.md#colour) covers the general rule.

## Solid colours only

Use the data-visualisation colours as they are, without opacity. A see-through mark has no fixed
contrast, because its colour depends on whatever is behind it, so it cannot be checked against the
goals above. The rest of the EDS colour scale follows the same rule. Instead of transparency:

- For a target range or an uncertainty band, use step 2 of the same hue, drawn behind the data, with a
  label.
- To fade the other bars or areas when one is selected, switch them to step 2. Lines are not faded:
  show or hide them instead.
- For overlapping areas, use stacked areas, lines or small multiples.
- For dense scatter plots, use smaller or hollow markers, or group the points into bins.

## Examples

The three charts below use only the tokens and rules on this page. The field names are Equinor
production fields, but every number is random example data with no connection to the production
of these fields.

### Several wells in one line chart

Gas rate, water rate and cumulative gas for six wells in three fields. Select the wells to show with
the checkboxes. Each measure has its own panel and its own scale, and the panels share one time axis.
Putting the three measures on one plot would need three y-axes, and lines on different scales cannot
be compared with each other.

Each well keeps its hue (`cat.1` to `cat.6`, step 4) in every panel, and keeps it when wells are
selected or cleared. Each well also has its own marker shape and a label at the end of its line.
Up to five wells, colour alone tells them apart. Select the sixth, and the shapes and labels do that
job. The end labels also cover `cat.1` in dark, where its step 4 is below the goal for lines. Move
the pointer over the chart, or focus it and use the arrow keys, to read all selected wells at one
date.

## Do's and Don'ts

:::info **Do**

- Choose the group from the data before choosing any colours.
- Keep a series on the same hue when a filter changes the chart.
- Add a second cue as soon as a chart has six or more series.
- Check every chart in light and dark.

:::

:::danger **Don't**

- Use `data-visualization.*` for interface elements such as buttons, banners or status badges. Use the semantic tone tokens for those.
- Use step 1 for anything that carries meaning, or step 1 or 2 for a line or a point.
- Use `data-visualization.*` for text.
- Make the colours see-through with opacity.

:::
